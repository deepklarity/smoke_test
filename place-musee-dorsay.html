Musée d'Orsay

A Belle Époque railway station transformed into the world's premier temple of Impressionist and Post-Impressionist masterpieces.

Back to all places

From Station to Sanctuary

The Gare d'Orsay opened in 1900 as Paris's first electrified railway station, serving southwestern France and welcoming visitors to the World's Fair. Its stunning Beaux-Arts architecture, with its soaring iron-and-glass barrel vault and elegant stone façade, was the work of three architects: Victor Laloux, Lucien Magne, and Émile Bénard. For nearly four decades, trains departed from beneath its magnificent central hall until declining rail traffic made the station obsolete.

By the 1970s, the derelict building faced demolition. A passionate campaign led by the museum's future director, Françoise Cachin, and supported by President Georges Pompidou, saved the structure. After an ambitious renovation by Gae Aulenti that transformed the station into a museum while preserving its architectural soul, the Musée d'Orsay opened in 1986. Today, it houses the world's largest collection of Impressionist and Post-Impressionist art — more than 3,000 works spanning 1848 to 1914.

What to See & Experience

The museum's collection reads like a who's-who of revolutionary 19th-century artists. You'll find Monet's "Women in the Garden," Renoir's "Bal du Moulin de la Galette," Van Gogh's "Starry Night Over the Rhône," and Cézanne's still lifes that would change the course of modern art. The fifth floor, bathed in natural light from the station's original skylights, displays the most iconic Impressionist works — the crowds here can be intense, but the masterpieces justify the wait.

Don't miss the museum's architectural highlights themselves. The former station's giant clock face on the fifth floor offers a surreal framing of Paris through its glass. The grand nave, once filled with locomotives, now features monumental sculptures including Rodin's "The Gates of Hell." The restored hotel rooms and ballroom showcase the opulence of the Belle Époque era. For a unique perspective, visit the museum's terrace — you'll see the Seine, the Louvre, and the dome of the Invalides all in one sweeping view.

Visitor Tips

Arrive at opening time (9:30am) or in late afternoon to avoid the worst crowds, especially on weekends and Tuesdays when the Louvre is closed. The museum's layout can be confusing — start on the top floor for the most famous Impressionist works, then descend through the lesser-known but equally fascinating collections on the lower floors. Temporary exhibitions require separate tickets and often sell out; book these online in advance.

The museum's café, located in the former station's Grand Hall with its ornate gilded ceiling and giant clock, offers one of Paris's most atmospheric lunch spots — though prices match the setting. Consider combining your visit with the nearby Musée de l'Orangerie, which houses Monet's water lily murals, with a joint ticket. And don't forget to look up — the station's original ironwork and the vast, light-filled vaulted ceiling are themselves masterpieces of industrial architecture.
