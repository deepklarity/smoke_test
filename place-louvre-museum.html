Louvre Museum

The world's largest and most visited art museum — a treasure trove spanning from ancient civilizations to the mid-19th century.

A Palace of Art Through the Ages

The Louvre began its life as a royal palace, built in 1190 by King Philip II as a fortress to defend the western edge of medieval Paris. Over the centuries, it served as a royal residence for Louis XIV before the court relocated to Versailles in 1682. The building's transformation into a public museum began in 1793, when the Revolutionary government opened the former royal collections to the people of France. Today, the Louvre welcomes over nine million visitors annually, making it the most visited museum on Earth.

The museum's collection is staggering in scope — nearly 380,000 objects are catalogued, with only about 35,000 on display at any time. The galleries span five millennia of human creativity, from Mesopotamian antiquities and Egyptian mummies to Greek sculpture and Italian Renaissance masterpieces. The most famous resident, Leonardo da Vinci's Mona Lisa, draws crowds daily to the Denon wing, where she gazes from behind bulletproof glass in the museum's most photographed room.

Highlights Not to Miss

Beyond the Mona Lisa, the Louvre offers countless other masterworks. The Winged Victory of Samothrace, a Hellenistic sculpture depicting the goddess Nike, stands majestically at the top of the Daru staircase, its marble drapery appearing to ripple in the wind. The Hammurabi Code stele, dating to 1754 BCE, offers a tangible connection to one of history's earliest legal systems. In the French galleries, Delacroix's monumental Liberty Leading the People captures the revolutionary spirit of 1830, while David's Oath of the Horatii exemplifies the Neoclassical style.

Architecture enthusiasts will appreciate the contrast between the historic palace wings and I.M. Pei's iconic glass pyramid, completed in 1989. The pyramid serves as the museum's main entrance and creates a striking contemporary counterpoint to the 17th-century buildings surrounding it. Beneath the pyramid lies the spacious Cour Napoléon, where visitors descend to the ticket counters and passageways connecting the museum's three main wings: Sully, Denon, and Richelieu.

Visitor Tips

Time your visit wisely — the Louvre is enormous, and attempting to see everything in one day is impossible. Focus on one or two wings per visit, and reserve at least three hours even for a condensed tour. Wednesday and Friday evenings offer a magical experience: the museum stays open until 9:45 PM, and the crowds thin considerably, particularly in the Italian galleries. Online timed-entry tickets are essential during peak season (April to October); book through the official Louvre website to guarantee entrance and skip the notoriously long queue at the pyramid.
