Eiffel Tower

The iron lady of Paris — a symbol of romance, engineering genius, and the enduring allure of the City of Light.

Back to all places

A Monument Born of Controversy

When Gustave Eiffel's wrought-iron lattice tower rose above the Champ de Mars for the 1889 Exposition Universelle, it was met with fierce opposition. Prominent artists and intellectuals — including Guy de Maupassant and Alexandre Dumas fils — signed a petition calling it a "useless monstrosity" that would disfigure the Paris skyline. Yet within months, the tower had become the darling of the public, drawing two million visitors in its first year alone. Today, it is the most visited paid monument in the world, welcoming nearly seven million guests annually.

Standing 330 metres tall (including its antenna), the Eiffel Tower held the title of the world's tallest man-made structure for 41 years until the Chrysler Building in New York surpassed it in 1930. Its three accessible levels offer progressively more spectacular views — from the sweeping panorama of Paris on the first two floors to the vertiginous summit, where on clear days you can see up to 70 kilometres.

What to See & Experience

The first and second levels house restaurants, a glass floor on the first level for the brave-hearted, and exhibits detailing the tower's history. The second floor's observation deck provides the sweet spot for photography — high enough for a sweeping vista, yet close enough to identify individual monuments like the Arc de Triomphe, Sacré-Cœur, and the Seine winding through the city.

The summit is reached by a separate elevator and rewards visitors with Gustave Eiffel's restored office, complete with wax figures of the engineer receiving Thomas Edison. At night, the tower sparkles with 20,000 bulbs for five minutes at the start of every hour after dusk — a spectacle that never fails to draw gasps from the crowds gathered on the Trocadéro gardens across the river.

Visitor Tips

Book tickets online well in advance — especially for summit access, which sells out days or weeks ahead. The best time to visit is late afternoon: you'll experience the tower in daylight, watch the sunset from the top, and see the city transform as the lights come on. If you're on a budget, the stairs to the second floor are significantly cheaper than the elevator and offer a unique perspective on the tower's intricate ironwork. Finally, the best free view of the tower is from the Trocadéro esplanade at sunrise, when the crowds are thin and the light is golden.
